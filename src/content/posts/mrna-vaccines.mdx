# RNA and DNA

DNA is the molecule that contains the genetic code of living organisms.

RNA is a class of molecules. There are various different types of RNA; mRNA (Messenger RNA) being one of them.

# Cytoplasm, ribosomes and the nucleus

![Human cell with nucleus and cytoplasm](/mrna-vaccines/human_cell.png)

The cytoplasm is the fluid inside the cell.

Ribosomes are used to create proteins and exist dispersed throughout the cytoplasm.

The nucleus contains all the cells DNA.

# mRNA

Messenger RNA is used in protein creation, where it carries the instructions from the DNA to the ribosomes.

# Cell division

One form of cell division occurs as such:
DNA is transcribed into mRNA inside the nucleus. This mRNA travels to the cytoplasm, where ribosomes read it and produce proteins.

# How does this information relate to the idea of mRNA vaccines?

We see that for mRNA vaccines to change our genes, it would have to penetrate the nucleus, and then selectively edit the DNA strands. However, mRNA is not capable of editing DNA, instead, as soon as mRNA finds it way into a cytoplasm, it will be read by ribosomes and used to create proteins. A single mRNA strand can be read multiple times, but will eventually degrade, whereupon the cell will recycle it into raw nucleotides.

# How do mRNA vaccines work?

mRNA encodes instructions for one specific protein. When we inject you with the vaccine, it enters the (generally, as its shot into the arm) muscle cell's cytoplasm and is picked up by the ribosomes, whereupon they produce, for awhile, a protein. The term 'spike protein' is specific to coronaviruses, but mRNA can be used to create any sort of protein. As long as the virus has proteins the immune system can see and remember and the virus doesn't mutate them too fast, you can make an effective mRNA vaccine.

# Different types of vaccines
### Whole inactivated
Whole-inactivated means the bacteria have been killed. This makes it very safe, and gives exposure to many parts of the virus, making for broad protection. However, it does not induce a strong immune response because heating the virus to death damages the viruses surface proteins and because it is not actually infecting you, the immune system does not react strongly. As well, creating them requires making lots of the unattenuated virus in a lab and killing it as the first step in the supply chain.

### Live-attenuated
Live-attenuated vaccines have a weakened version of the virus in them. They are cost effective to make and give a strong immune response. However, they are difficult to distribute because of strict refrigeration requirements.

### Protein subunit
Protein subunit vaccines use (kind of like mRNA vaccines) a specific protein of the virus. This is extremely safe compared to live-attenuated viruses, which may not be safe for immunocompromised individuals. They do not produce the strongest immune response however, and normally require boosters. Their biggest weakness however, is their manufacturing time AND cost, which is the highest of all vaccines. They are manufactured by inserting the genetic code for a protein into yeast cells, which then make the protein. This is a complex process, requiring purification steps and large vats called bioreactors.<Ref n={1} />

### mRNA
mRNA vaccines use mRNA to instruct cells to make a viral protein. They are very safe like protein subunit vaccines, however they are much FASTER to manufacture (mRNA can be made purely from chemical reactions, though this is not necessarily cheap). However, because your own cells make the protein, your body reacts more strongly than subunit, knowing that its ribosomes have been hijacked. As well, the payload is larger than subunit, because mRNA cells take hours to days to degrade within the cell, giving time for lots of viral protein to be made. mRNA vaccines have strict cold chain supply line requirements (which is why once they're opened they have to be used or binned within a short period of time). They cannot be used to make every part of the virus and so some parts of the immune system cannot be utilised by them. They also tend to cause more side-effects like sore arms and fatigue (because the immune system becomes busy).

# Death from covid vaccines

We must remember first of all that there were several different types of Covid vaccines produced. AstraZeneca, Pfizer/BioNTech, Sinovac, Moderna, and Sinopharm.<Ref n={3} /> Of these, <b>only</b> Pfizer and Moderna were mRNA vaccines.

Like all medicines, COVID-19 vaccines can cause side effects. Most are mild — sore arm, headache, fever — but a few rare serious ones have been linked to specific platforms. AstraZeneca has been associated with thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and immune thrombocytopenia.<Ref n={4} /> Pfizer and Moderna have been linked to myocarditis and pericarditis.<Ref n={5} /> When attributing a death to vaccination, it matters which vaccine was used. As of December 2021, Australia's TGA had identified 11 deaths linked to AstraZeneca vaccination — eight from thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome, two from Guillain-Barré syndrome, and one from immune thrombocytopenia.<Ref n={4} /> The protective benefits of vaccination continue to outweigh these risks. For context, COVID-19 itself had caused roughly 7 million reported deaths worldwide as of April 2024.<Ref n={6} />

# How does the immune system produce a response to protein?

## Protein subunit injections and immune system responses

Lets walk through a something slightly simpler than mRNA: a nurse injects a protein subunit vaccine into your arm. Nothing else — just viral protein in salty water. The same basic immune story applies here; mRNA mainly changes how much protein the body sees, and for how long.

### Seconds

Seconds after the needle has been inserted, plunged, and withdrawn, the bodies first lines of defence begin to activate. Mast cells, affected by molecular signalling, release histamine, causing the blood vessels to swell and become more permeable. This is the sore, warm, slightly swollen arm — and it happens partly because of the needle, not just the vaccine.

### Minutes

The vaccine liquid spreads through the fluid between your muscle cells. Immune cells already living there encounter the viral protein. A dendritic cell — one of the important ones — takes the protein in and breaks it into tiny fragments called peptides. It then displays those peptides on its surface, like badges showing what it found. If the inflammation is strong enough, that cell leaves the arm and travels through lymphatic vessels — drainage channels that run parallel to blood vessels — toward a lymph node. For an arm injection, that often means the armpit.

### Hours

A lymph node is where the immune system holds briefings. T cells circulate through constantly, each built to recognise a different shape. The dendritic cell waits until a T cell whose receptor fits the displayed peptide stops. If the match is confirmed, that one T cell begins to divide — over hours and days, becoming a small army of copies, all trained to recognise the same viral protein. Those activated T cells then seek out B cells and help them start making antibodies — proteins that stick to the virus and mark it for destruction. That antibody production takes days. But the chain is complete: protein in the arm → learning in the lymph node → antibodies in the blood. The vaccine gave the immune system a safe preview of one part of the virus.

### A note on statistics

The goal of statistics in this exercise will be to help us understand which vaccine was better. For that we need to understand who got sick more often and whether they got sicker. To assess this, we use an abbreviation IRR, it stands for Incidence Rate Ratio, and is calculated to assess the ratio of new events between groups.

All it is is you take how many people in a population got sick, compared with how many people in a different population got sick, and then you compare them:

<Quote>Group A Count / Group B Count = X</Quote>

So if X was 2, Group A would have twice as many people who got sick.

### IRR of Sinovac vs Moderna vs Pfizer

So its pretty simple, for those who received 2 shots of:

<Quote>After adjusting for age, gender, ethnicity, residency status, socioeconomic status, time since second dose, and daily infection rate, individuals vaccinated with Sinovac-CoronaVac were more likely to be infected (adjusted IRR, 2.37; 95% CI, 2.29–2.46), and more likely to develop severe disease (adjusted IRR, 4.59; 95% CI, 3.25–6.48); individuals vaccinated with Sinopharm were also at higher risk of infection (adjusted IRR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.43–1.85), while individuals vaccinated with Moderna were at lower risk of severe disease (adjusted IRR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.25–0.70), compared with those who received Pfizer-BioNTech/Comirnaty (Table 1).<Ref n={7}/></Quote>

Okay, lets just digest that:

After adjustment, Sinovac recipients had roughly 2.4× the infection rate of Pfizer recipients.

They were also much more likely to get severe COVID: about 4.6× the rate of severe disease compared with Pfizer. The gap for severe disease is larger than for infection.

# Why do different vaccines require different intervals between reapplication?

This is a great question. There are two things that dictate vaccination intervals, the first is how fast the virus tends to mutate. For Covid, that was relatively fast. But tetanus specifically was brought up and we can treat it as a special case: tetanus only requires a vaccine rarely because tetanus bacteria mutate very slowly. In fact, a paper from 2017 compared tetanus bacteria from 1962 to now and found:

<Quote>The analyses indicate that Harvard vaccine strains are remarkably similar... No mutations were observed in TetR or TetX providing assurance that genetic drift has not compromised tetanus toxin antigenicity used in vaccine manufacture.<Ref n={8} /></Quote>

So it doesn't mutate that often.

The second thing that drives vaccination rates is the strength of the immune reaction. Now this is where we have to get into specifics. Because the tetanus vaccine doesn't actually vaccinate against the bacteria at all. It vaccinates against a toxin that the bacteria produce. They chemically inactivate it and introduce it to the body. The tetanus toxin in fact a protein<Ref n={9} />, so in fact, this can be considered a form of subunit vaccine.

Why vaccinate against the toxin? It reliably produces a strong immune response when coupled with an inflammatory formulation that heightens the immune response, and because the toxin kills you quicker than the infection, and I guess that its just lucky that the toxin is specifically a protein, which our immune system's special cells can interact with easily.

So basically, for COVID, much like the flu, it simply mutates quite fast an for awhile was deadly enough that getting boosters to new variants was good medical advice. Although its still good medical advice now, much like getting your flu shot, we are lucky that it has mutated to a less deadly disease.
